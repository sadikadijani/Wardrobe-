A light, free-standing wardrobe you can build with a drill and Hornbach's cutting service: hanging space above (one open space or split in two), two open boxes below for shoes and folded clothes, on a base frame with legs tall enough for a robot vacuum. Checks

How the child test works: a 27 kg child (the 60 lb test weight in the US furniture tip-over standard ASTM F2057-23) is modelled two ways. First, hanging from the top front edge and leaning back, which pulls outward with half their weight. Second, standing on the outer edge of an open door. Both are checked with the wardrobe empty. This is a calculation to size the parts, not a certified test, so try it yourself once it's built (see the last build step).

      Which wood to buy

      Poplar plywood, 18 mm pick this

      German: Pappelsperrholz. About 400–450 kg/m³, so roughly a third lighter than chipboard. Screws hold well and you can take it apart and rebuild it many times. Sand the edges, then oil or paint it.

      Melamine chipboard, 19 mm

      German: Spanplatte beschichtet / Möbelbauplatte. Cheapest option and comes already finished, but heaviest (~650–700 kg/m³). Ask Hornbach to edge-band the front edges (Kantenumleimung). Screw holes wear out if you take it apart often.

      Birch plywood, 18 mm

      German: Birke Multiplex. Stiffest, with the nicest visible edges, but heavy and roughly twice the price of poplar. Only worth it if you want bare wood as the finished look.

      For the back, use 3 mm hardboard (HDF / Hartfaserplatte, white on one side). It weighs almost nothing and is the part that stops the wardrobe from wobbling sideways. For the base frame, use planed spruce battens 44 × 44 mm (Rahmenholz gehobelt). The stability comes from a longer base frame and removable concrete slabs hidden in the boxes, not from heavier wood. That keeps every piece you carry light and cheap.
